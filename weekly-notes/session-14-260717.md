# Session 14 — Posterior-Alpha Window-Length and Step-Size Comparison

* **Planned period:** 2026-07-16–2026-07-18
* **Actual work dates:** 2026-07-17–2026-07-22
* **Status:** Completed

This session continued the public-data analysis developed across Sessions 07 and 13. Session 07 established the EEGBCI eyes-open/eyes-closed posterior spectral-analysis workflow and condition-level alpha-power comparison. Session 13 extended this workflow to a sliding-window posterior-alpha feature time series. Session 14 evaluated the sensitivity of that feature stream to outer-window length and step size and selected a baseline configuration for subsequent decision-rule analysis.

## 1. Objectives

* Quantify within-run variability of the posterior-alpha feature for the eyes-open and eyes-closed baseline recordings.
* Compare feature behavior across 1 s, 2 s, and 4 s outer-window lengths with a fixed 1 s step.
* Compare temporal update patterns across 0.5 s, 1 s, and 2 s steps with a fixed 2 s outer window.
* Quantify the separation between the two recordings under each configuration.
* Select an initial feature-update configuration for the Session 15 offline decision-rule analysis.

## 2. Research Questions

### Main question

How do outer-window length and step size affect the variability, temporal representation, and eyes-open/eyes-closed separation of the posterior-alpha feature stream?

### Subquestions

1. How does outer-window length affect within-run variability and the separation between the two recordings?
2. How does step size affect update density and the visibility of intermediate temporal excursions?
3. Which configuration provides an appropriate initial balance for the Session 15 offline decision-rule analysis?

## 3. Analysis Scope and Configuration

### 3.1 Dataset

* **Dataset:** PhysioNet EEG Motor Movement/Imagery Dataset, accessed through MNE EEGBCI
* **Subject:** 1
* **Run 1:** baseline eyes open
* **Run 2:** baseline eyes closed
* **Sampling frequency:** 160 Hz
* **Target interval:** T0 annotation
* **Analyzed duration:** windows fully contained within the first 60 s of each target interval

### 3.2 Preprocessing

* Continuous-run band-pass filtering: 1–40 Hz
* Filtering was applied before outer-window extraction.
* Posterior channels:

  * `Po3.`
  * `Poz.`
  * `Po4.`
  * `O1..`
  * `Oz..`
  * `O2..`

### 3.3 Welch PSD and Feature Definition

* Welch window: Hann
* Segment length: 160 samples, equivalent to 1 s
* Segment overlap: 80 samples, equivalent to 0.5 s
* FFT length: 160 samples
* Detrending: constant
* PSD scaling: density
* Segment aggregation: arithmetic mean
* Alpha band: (8 \leq f < 13) Hz
* Alpha bins: 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12 Hz

For each outer window:

1. Welch PSD was calculated for each posterior channel.
2. PSD was averaged across the six posterior channels at each frequency.
3. The posterior-channel mean PSD was averaged across the alpha-frequency bins.

The resulting feature was `posterior_alpha_mean_psd`, expressed in (V^2/Hz).

Raw PSD values were used for calculations. Logarithmic scaling was used only for visualization.

A window was included when:

[
window_start + window_length \leq run_duration
]

### 3.5 Summary and Comparison Metrics

Within-run variability was summarized using:

* median
* Q25 and Q75
* interquartile range
* IQR divided by median
* mean
* population standard deviation
* minimum and maximum
* Q05 and Q95

The two recordings were compared using:

[
\text{Median ratio} =
\frac{\text{EC median}}{\text{EO median}}
]

[
G_{90} =
\text{EC Q05} - \text{EO Q95}
]

[
G_{\text{range}} =
\text{EC minimum} - \text{EO maximum}
]

Pairwise ordering was calculated as:

[
P(EC > EO) + 0.5P(EC = EO)
]

### 4.4 Auxiliary Local Decomposition

Visual inspection of the window-length comparison figure showed a marked divergence among the 1 s, 2 s, and 4 s configurations near 25 s in the eyes-open recording.

`scripts/09b_eegbci_local_window_decomposition.py` was added to inspect the Welch-segment composition of this interval.

The 23–27 s interval was decomposed into seven overlapping 1 s Welch segments with a 0.5 s segment step. Segment-level posterior-alpha estimates were recalculated and compared with the feature values saved by the main pipeline.

The mean of the three segments within the 24–26 s window reproduced the stored 2 s feature. The mean of all seven segments within the 23–27 s window reproduced the stored 4 s feature.

The 1 s configuration showed the largest relative within-run variability in both recordings.

The 4 s configuration showed lower relative variability and the largest central-range separation. It was the only configuration with a positive minimum-to-maximum separation.

The 2 s configuration retained positive central-range separation while using an intermediate temporal support and three Welch segments per feature estimate.

### 5.2 Step-Size Comparison

With the window length fixed at 2 s:

| Step  | Overlap | Outputs per run | Update structure                  |
| ----- | ------: | --------------: | --------------------------------- |
| 0.5 s |     75% |             117 | Densest update grid               |
| 1.0 s |     50% |              59 | Intermediate update grid          |
| 2.0 s |      0% |              30 | Non-overlapping and sparsest grid |

Feature values were identical at shared 2 s window positions.

The 0.5 s configuration added estimates at half-second intermediate positions. These additional positions exposed peaks and dips that were absent from the coarser output streams.

The 2 s step produced non-overlapping feature estimates but provided only 30 updates across the recording.

## 6. Interpretation

### 6.1 Window Length and Temporal Integration

Longer outer windows integrated a broader temporal interval and a larger number of Welch segments. The 1 s configuration retained the greatest local variability, whereas the 4 s configuration produced a more stable feature trajectory and stronger distribution separation.

The increased stability of the 4 s feature was accompanied by a longer first-availability time and greater temporal averaging. The 2 s configuration occupied an intermediate position between the higher variability of the 1 s setting and the broader integration of the 4 s setting.

### 6.2 Step Size and Temporal Representation

The identical values at shared window positions confirmed that changing step size did not alter the estimator applied to a given 2 s interval.

Smaller steps added estimates at intermediate temporal positions and made additional excursions visible in the feature stream. For threshold-based control, these intermediate estimates may increase the number of observable short-duration crossings. They also increase overlap and temporal dependence between consecutive updates.

The 1 s step retained the main temporal pattern with an intermediate update density and 50% overlap.

### 6.3 Local Scale and Alignment Sensitivity

The local divergence near 25 s was associated with a high-valued half-step segment that was included in the 2 s and 4 s Welch estimates but was not represented as an independent point in the 1 s / 1 s output stream.

The event had a strong influence on the 2 s estimate because it contributed one of three Welch segments. Its contribution was reduced in the 4 s estimate because it was averaged with six additional segments.

This local case showed that the appearance of a short-lived event in the feature stream depended on temporal support, segment alignment, and aggregation scale.

## 7. Decision

The following configuration was selected as **baseline configuration v0.1** for Session 15:

```text
Window length: 2.0 s
Step size: 1.0 s
Outer-window overlap: 50%
Welch segments per feature: 3
First feature availability: 2.0 s
```

Selection basis:

* lower relative within-run variability than the 1 s window;
* shorter temporal integration and earlier availability than the 4 s window;
* lower output redundancy than the 0.5 s step;
* denser update cadence than the non-overlapping 2 s step;
* positive central-range separation between the two analyzed recordings.
